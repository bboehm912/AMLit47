02   /   Context

A dream under
pressure.

Arthur Miller's Death of a Salesman premiered in 1949, amid a changing postwar United States. It follows salesman Willy Loman and his family as they navigate work, expectations, and belonging.

Rather than treating success as a simple destination, the play asks what happens when a person's sense of worth becomes entangled with the idea of being successful.

Is success something we achieve, something others recognize, or something we inherit from the culture around us?

A lens for reading

The American Dream

A cultural idea that opportunity and prosperity are possible through effort. Who has access to that promise? Who defines what counts as success?

PERSONAL

Identity

How does a person want to be seen?

FAMILIAL

Expectation

How do dreams move between parents and children?

CULTURAL

Value

Who or what decides what a person is worth?

04   /   Historical context

Who gets to frame the story?

The play prompted debate, but its reception was not simply negative: it won the 1949 Pulitzer Prize for Drama. A different controversy surrounded the 1951 film adaptation.

THE STUDIO

A reassuring short

Columbia Pictures commissioned a short called Career of a Salesman to accompany its 1951 film. It presented a positive picture of salesmanship and suggested Willy was not representative of the modern salesman.

THE PLAYWRIGHT

Miller objected

Miller saw the short as undermining his work and threatened legal action. Columbia agreed to withdraw it. The dispute is about the framing of the film, not proof that the original play was broadly rejected.

YOUR INTERPRETATION

A question of power

Does defending the profession shift attention away from the play's critique of aspiration and social values? What evidence would support or complicate that claim?

Historical sources: Kevin Kerrane, “Arthur Miller vs. Columbia Pictures” and The Pulitzer Prizes: Arthur Miller. The title is Career of a Salesman, sometimes confused with “Life of a Salesman.”

05   /   Disillusioned modernity

The modern self
in the city.

Compare urban and cultural identities across verse and prose, then bring those insights to Miller's theater. Modernist writing often experiments with voice and form to represent a world that feels fragmented or uncertain.

How does the city shape the way a person sees themselves—and how others see them?

Core objective

Compare lyrical modernism with theatrical realism.

Explain how the choices of each form develop a related idea about identity, belonging, or disillusionment.

VERSE

Listen to a voice

In a class-selected Modernist poem, notice speaker, imagery, rhythm, and the speaker's relationship to the city.

PROSE

Trace a perspective

In a class-selected prose passage, examine narration, setting, and details of cultural identity.

THEATER

Watch a world

In Miller's opening, consider dialogue, stage space, performance, and the pressures of everyday life.

Compare a specific detail from each text. Notice that Miller's realism also blends present action with memory; the forms are not rigid opposites.

06   /   Ego and collapse

When aspiration
becomes identity.

Bring Miller into conversation with Steinbeck and other mid-century texts from your course. Consider how individuals pursue a better life while facing social and economic pressures.

When a dream is difficult to attain, should we question the individual, the dream, or the conditions surrounding both?

Core objective

Build an argumentative claim across a diverse corpus.

Make a debatable comparison; support it with precise evidence from Miller and a class-selected Steinbeck text, and consider a meaningful difference.

COMPARATIVE THINKING

Look for a tension

What do characters hope for? What pressures or assumptions shape those hopes? Where do the texts agree—and where do they diverge?

CLAIM FRAME

Make it arguable

While Miller presents aspiration as ________, Steinbeck suggests ________; together, the texts complicate the belief that ________.

Before we go
any further.

Return to the film's opening. Use what you actually observed, not what you expect the story to become.

Exit ticket

What tension between personal ambition and society do you expect this play to explore?
